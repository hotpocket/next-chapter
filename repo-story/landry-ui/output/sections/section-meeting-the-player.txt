Meeting the player.

landry-ui is a component library written in plain JavaScript with no build step, and its audiobook player is the component playing this narration right now. This book walks through it in four chapters. This one covers the player's core: the library screen, the audio model, and the transport. The second covers the reading surface, the third offline listening, the fourth integration and tests.

One honesty note, stated once. Everything in this chapter was built between the second of April and the sixteenth of July, twenty twenty-six, and this project only began exporting its prompts on the twentieth of July. No verbatim prompt survives for any feature here. The provenance is the commit message, paraphrased, plus a session note from early April that summarizes the work. Where even a commit is silent, the attribution is inference. None of what follows is quoted as the user's words.

The player opens on the library view, drawn by a function named renderLibrary. Each book gets one meta line — its chapter count, a middle dot, its total duration — and a small progress dot: gray for untouched, blue for in progress, green once the book is more than ninety-eight percent complete. The heading is configurable; the calling page passes a title, and if it passes none, the word audiobook stands in. That fallback, and a fix so auto-resume stops overriding the library screen, land together in a commit from the second of April, and the commit message is the only record of the instruction.

Resume sits underneath the library. The player writes your position to the browser's local storage every five seconds, again when you leave the page, and again each time a chapter ends. Two keys carry it: rs-progress, suffixed with the book's index, holds the book time and the fraction complete; rs-last-book points at the book you had open. Load the page with no route and the last book reopens where you left it, paused. The session note records one rule as a mistake to avoid: returning to the library must delete the last-book pointer and forget the current book, or the five-second save quietly writes the pointer back and pulls you into the book again.

Now the audio model, the pivotal decision of the component. A book is not one file. It is one small M4A per chapter, and a single audio element loads exactly one chapter at a time. The change lands in a commit from the twenty-sixth of April. The reason survives only downstream: a later repo-story session recap records that the player switched to per-chapter files and that its own build scripts lagged behind the change — a witness describing the consequence, not the instruction, so the why is inference. Because one chapter is loaded, the audio element's own clock is chapter-local. The player builds a book-wide clock on top of it: the chapter's start offset plus the local time. Finding which chapter contains a given book time is a binary search over the chapter starts.

That model shows up on screen as the chapter panel. Each chapter is a row with its title, its duration, a progress fill that animates only while that chapter is active, and a scrubber handle; dragging the active chapter's handle seeks live, while dragging any other chapter's handle loads that chapter from its start on release. The list keeps the active row parked about a third of the way down the pane, and stops steering the moment you scroll it yourself. The panel also declares itself a CSS container: squeezed below one hundred and fifty pixels wide, the titles and durations disappear and each row collapses to the label Ch plus a number. That number is the row's position in the list, which matches the chapter's own number whenever the book is sequential. The session note describes this compaction as a built feature — a summary of the work, not a quote.

Next, the transport bar along the bottom: minus thirty seconds, previous chapter, play and pause, next chapter, plus thirty seconds, and a speed button. Skipping preserves whether you were playing or paused. The speed button cycles a fixed list — zero point seven five, one, one and a quarter, one and a half, one and three quarters, two — starting at normal speed, wrapping around, and deliberately not remembered between sessions.

Above those buttons is the track bar. Grabbing it takes pointer capture, so the drag keeps working after your finger or cursor strays off the thin bar. While you drag, the fill and the time readout follow you continuously, and if the target sits inside the loaded chapter the audio seeks in real time; only on release into a different chapter does the player load that chapter's file, because loading on every move would thrash the audio. Small ticks mark where each chapter begins. One verified subtlety: the drag math is still computed against the full book duration, while the ticks and the passive progress fill follow the summary-aware clock that chapter two introduces — in summary mode the drag and the fill disagree. That is what the shipped code does, and no test narrates it. The two commits behind the bar, both from the sixteenth of July, say drag-to-seek across chapters and hover thumb riding the progress edge — the commit's words, from a push that predates prompt exporting.

Last, the address bar is itself a control. Opening a book pushes a short hash route — a slash and the book's slug, lowercased and hyphenated from its title — so the position is shareable, and an empty hash means the library. Browser back and forward move the player with them, and on load a matching hash outranks the auto-resume pointer. The routing arrives whole in one commit from the sixth of May, and only its subject line records the intent.

Sound is half the player. The words on screen are the other half.
